All five challenges are deeply important and interconnected. However, I would place improving global preparedness for future pandemics first because of their potential to cause immediate, widespread, and long-lasting damage. COVID-19 showed how quickly an outbreak can overwhelm healthcare systems, disrupt economies, and affect everyday life across the world. More importantly, a future pandemic could be significantly more contagious or deadly. Investing in preparedness today, through early detection, stronger healthcare systems, resilient supply chains, and better international coordination—could prevent a local outbreak from becoming a global catastrophe.

A future pandemic is not a distant possibility, it is an inevitability. The real question is whether the world will be better prepared when it arrives. COVID-19 showed that scientific capability alone is not enough. Countries with advanced healthcare systems still struggled because warning signals were missed, decisions were delayed, healthcare workers were overwhelmed, and essential supplies were unavailable when they were needed most.

If I had a $100 million grant, I would focus on building a stronger global pandemic preparedness system that can detect outbreaks earlier, respond faster, and protect vulnerable populations before a local outbreak becomes a global crisis.

1. preparedness requires strong and decisive leadership ($10M) :- During a pandemic, uncertainty can quickly turn into confusion. Governments need clear decision-making structures that connect national, regional, and local authorities. Early warning data is only valuable if someone can interpret it and act quickly. Leaders must also communicate honestly with the public about the risks, the decisions being made, and why those decisions matter. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated how different leadership approaches could dramatically influence outcomes. Countries such as South Korea showed the value of rapid testing, contact tracing, isolation, and coordinated public communication.

2. the world needs better early-warning and disease surveillance systems ($30M) :- Detecting an outbreak days or weeks earlier can potentially save thousands or even millions of lives. This means investing in surveillance infrastructure, rapid diagnostic technologies, laboratory capacity, and systems that allow health authorities to quickly share information. Early detection should not only identify individual cases but also recognize unusual patterns, emerging clusters, and populations at higher risk. A strong surveillance system can give governments the time needed to contain an outbreak before it spreads internationally.

3. healthcare workers must be treated as critical infrastructure ($20M) :- During COVID-19, doctors, nurses, laboratory staff, and community health workers carried an enormous burden. They treated patients, conducted testing, delivered vaccines, reported cases, and continued providing essential healthcare services. Yet many healthcare systems entered the pandemic already understaffed and underprepared. Pandemic preparedness should therefore include long-term investment in recruiting, training, and protecting healthcare workers. Building surge capacity before a crisis is far more effective than trying to create it in the middle of one.

4. global logistics and supply chains need to become more resilient ($15M) :- A pandemic response depends on much more than vaccines or medicines. Healthcare workers need protective equipment. Testing requires diagnostic kits and laboratory supplies. Vaccination campaigns require syringes, transportation, trained personnel, and reliable cold-chain infrastructure. During COVID-19, shortages and competition between countries exposed how fragile global supply systems could be. Preparedness should include regional stockpiles, diversified manufacturing, and logistics systems that can rapidly distribute essential resources where they are needed most.

5. pandemic preparedness must be tested before the next pandemic begins ($15M) :- Governments regularly conduct military exercises to prepare for potential conflicts, but pandemic preparedness often remains a document that is rarely tested in realistic conditions. Countries and international organizations should regularly conduct simulations involving different pathogens, transmission routes, and worst-case scenarios. COVID-19 also revealed unexpected challenges, including superspreading events and airborne transmission. Preparing for only the last pandemic is not enough; planning must account for multiple possible futures.

Finally, pandemic preparedness must adopt a genuinely global approach. Infectious diseases do not respect national borders, and no country can fully protect itself in isolation. Human health is closely connected to animal health, environmental conditions, agriculture, and global mobility. A stronger preparedness strategy should therefore follow a `One Health approach` ($10M), bringing together public health experts, veterinarians, environmental scientists, governments, and international organizations. Investments should support surveillance, prevention, vaccine research, and healthcare capacity in lower-resource countries as well as wealthier ones.

If I were allocating $100 million, I would not try to build another large emergency-response organization. Instead, I would invest in the gaps that repeatedly cause outbreaks to become catastrophes: early detection, trained healthcare workers, resilient supply chains, realistic preparedness exercises, and faster coordination between countries.

The goal would be simple: make the next outbreak visible earlier, understandable faster, and containable before it becomes a pandemic.

In my professional work, I worked on a multi-agent system for generating business insights from data. The goal was to allow users to ask business questions in natural language and receive analysis, visualisations, and actionable next steps.

I initially designed the system with specialised agents for routing, business logic, SQL generation, analytical reasoning, Plotly visualisation, and recommendations. My assumption was that separating responsibilities across agents would make the system more reliable and easier to reason about.

In practice, this introduced a different problem: orchestration overhead. Simple questions were sometimes passed through multiple agents unnecessarily, increasing latency and creating opportunities for context to be lost between agents. I also encountered cases where the SQL was technically valid but did not answer the intended business question, or where the generated visualisation looked reasonable but did not support the actual analysis.

I investigated these failures by testing the system with different business queries and examining the intermediate outputs rather than only evaluating the final response. I tightened the boundaries between agents, improved the routing logic, structured the information passed between agents, and avoided invoking specialised agents when they were not necessary. These changes reduced typical end-to-end latency from roughly 1–2 minutes to around 8–15 seconds.

The biggest change in my thinking was that adding more agents does not necessarily make an AI system better. The interfaces between agents can be more important than the agents themselves. I had initially focused on agent capability: I became more focused on orchestration, verification, and deciding when not to use an agent.

If I were building it again, I would define evaluation criteria earlier for routing accuracy, SQL correctness, analytical validity, visualisation usefulness, and end-to-end latency. I would also establish a baseline using a simpler single-agent architecture before introducing multi-agent complexity.

The problem is still not fully solved. I am particularly interested in how to determine the right level of agent decomposition and how to reliably measure whether an AI-generated business insight is actually useful for decision-making, rather than simply being plausible.
